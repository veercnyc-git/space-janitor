About SpaceJanitor

A mission planner for orbital debris removal. Built by 8th graders.

The Problem — Kessler Syndrome

In 1978, NASA scientist Donald Kessler proposed a nightmare scenario: if the density of objects in Low Earth Orbit reaches a critical threshold, a single collision could trigger a cascading chain reaction — creating ever more debris until entire orbital shells become unusable for generations.

Today there are 27,000+ tracked objects and hundreds of thousands of smaller untracked fragments. Major events like Fengyun 1C (2007) and the Iridium/Cosmos collision (2009) have already seeded dense debris clouds at critical altitudes.

The Math — Hohmann Transfers

To move a spacecraft between two circular orbits efficiently, we use a Hohmann transfer: two engine burns tracing an elliptical arc between the orbits. The fuel cost (delta-v) is:

Δv₁ = √(μ/r₁) × (√(2r₂/(r₁+r₂)) − 1)
Δv₂ = √(μ/r₂) × (1 − √(2r₁/(r₁+r₂)))

Where μ = 3.986×10¹⁴ m³/s². Our AI scores every possible collection ordering using these equations and finds the cheapest total route.

About the Orbital View

The 3D globe shows each object's orbital shell — the correct altitude and inclination from real Celestrak data. However, the dot position along the orbit is representative, not real-time. Calculating exact positions requires SGP4 orbital propagation math, which accounts for atmospheric drag, Earth's oblateness, and solar pressure. That's college-level stuff — the shells are real, the dots are approximations.

Data Source

Orbital data comes from Celestrak's GP catalog (maintained by Dr. T.S. Kelso — the standard reference used by space agencies worldwide), hosted in our GitHub repo as catalog.json.
